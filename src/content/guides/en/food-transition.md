## Understand the current intake first

Observe the bird's familiar food, preferred bowl and normal eating routine over ordinary days. A full-looking bowl is not evidence of intake; remove husks and check edible food. With multiple birds, watch whether each has access.

Do not improvise dietary conversion for a sick, weak, unweaned or newly arrived bird that is not eating normally. Plan with a veterinarian experienced with birds first.

## Offer the new food alongside familiar food

Present a small amount in a separate clean bowl. Changing many ingredients together makes acceptance harder to interpret. Suitable vegetables can be offered in different safe cuts or textures; rejecting one presentation does not mean permanent rejection of the ingredient.

Looking, approaching, touching with the beak and actually swallowing are different steps. Encourage interest calmly. Do not force food into the mouth, punish refusal or starve the bird into accepting it.

## Check pellet intake rather than the packet

Choose formulated food appropriate to the species and individual; consider particle size and the ability to eat it. Pecking at pellets does not prove adequate intake. Check the bowl and floor to distinguish eaten food from scattered powder.

Do not abruptly remove familiar food. Adjust steps according to actual acceptance and condition instead of forcing a universal completion deadline. Obtain veterinary help with the individual's feeding plan.

## Keep a simple record

Record what was offered and eaten, behaviour and whether droppings remain typical. Voluntary use of a suitable gram scale is useful. Comparable observations make changes easier to interpret; another bird's weight is not a target for yours.

Fruit and water intake can alter the liquid in droppings. Look for reduced eating, lethargy and other signs rather than diagnosing from colour. If intake falls, seek help instead of withholding familiar food more strictly.

## Observe companions separately

One bird accepting the new food does not prove another is eating it. Provide multiple feeding positions and watch for chasing, guarding bowls and poor intake. Do not combine a newcomer or quarantined bird with others just to demonstrate feeding.

## When assessment takes priority

Reduced eating, fewer droppings, weakness or loss of normal daily activity is not simply stubbornness. Breathing difficulty, collapse, severe weakness or suspected toxins need urgent assessment. Do not guess a medication or supplement treatment.

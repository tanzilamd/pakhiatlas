## Three components, one dropping

A typical dropping contains feces from the digestive tract, usually opaque urates, and liquid urine. On absorbent paper the liquid may spread around the other parts. The proportions and appearance vary with species, diet and water intake. The diagram below is schematic, not a clinical photograph.

## Wet does not always mean diarrhea

An increased urine component can surround otherwise formed feces. True diarrhea concerns loss of fecal form. Fruit intake, stress and other factors may alter appearance, but a persistent change must not be dismissed as diet. A veterinarian may need history, examination and tests to distinguish causes.

## Look for patterns, not a diagnosis

Food pigments may change fecal colour. Changes in urate colour, new foul odour, undigested food or consistently unusual texture need assessment. Colour alone cannot identify an infection, liver disorder or any other disease. Never start an antibiotic or supplement based on a photograph.

## When to seek urgent help

Blood or suspected blood, black tar-like material, markedly reduced droppings with poor intake, straining, collapse or breathing difficulty need prompt veterinary advice. Lethargy alongside a persistent change increases concern. Do not wait for an online colour match. Do not force food or fluid into a sick bird.

## Make your observations useful

Use clean plain paper beneath the usual resting area. Note recent food, time, appetite, behavior and whether several consecutive droppings changed. Take a clear photograph in natural light. Ask the clinic whether and how to bring a fresh sample; do not delay transport to collect one. Wash hands and keep preparation surfaces separate from human food.

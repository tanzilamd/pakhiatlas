## Planning cockatiel meals

This guide covers choosing suitable staple food, vegetables and limited extras for cockatiels. Whole-diet principles are in [balanced feeding](../balanced-diet/); seed ratios, shopping and stock arithmetic are in the [seed-mix planner](../seed-mix/).

## Rotate a small vegetable selection

Washed broccoli, carrot, pumpkin and pesticide-free coriander are useful options to introduce. They need not all be mixed together. Try different safe presentations without salt, oil, sauces or spices.

Spinach is not universally forbidden. Small amounts can be rotated with other suitable greens; it should not be the only vegetable or the assumed calcium source. Identify any plant and its clean source before offering it.

## Fruit is an extra, not the staple

Small portions of apple flesh, ripe papaya, watermelon or banana can be occasional fruit. Remove apple seeds and core, and stone-fruit pits. Not every seed in every fruit has the same toxicology; offering separated flesh is a straightforward habit. Watery fruit can increase liquid in droppings, but that change alone does not diagnose disease.

A large piece of fruit can be a lot for a small bird. Review portions if sweet foods displace the staple diet.

## Chickpeas, mung beans and egg

Overnight soaking does not sterilise legumes or make them equivalent to cooked food. Warm, wet conditions can support microbial growth. Offer small portions of thoroughly cooked plain legumes and remove them before spoilage. Sprouting requires its own strict hygiene; discard sour, slimy or moldy batches entirely.

Plain thoroughly cooked egg can be a limited extra in some feeding plans, but healthy adult pets do not universally need it twice weekly. Do not automatically increase protein or supplements for every molt or breeding attempt.

## Grit, cuttlebone and mineral blocks

Cockatiels hull seeds and generally do not need insoluble grit for digestion. Do not copy pigeon-feeding practice. A soluble calcium source such as cuttlebone is different from insoluble stone grit.

A block does not make an otherwise incomplete diet balanced. Do not automatically add iron, iodine or calcium without considering the existing food and the bird’s condition. Discuss laying and calcium concerns with an avian veterinarian.

## Routine care instead of herbal medicine schedules

Neem or tulsi twice monthly should not be treated as preventive medical care. Plant identity, preparation and species matter; herbal remedies must not delay assessment of illness. Clean water, appropriate food, safe air, rest and early illness recognition are the practical foundation.

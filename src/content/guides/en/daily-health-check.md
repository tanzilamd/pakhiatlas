## Look at the whole bird

Watch appetite, activity, posture, breathing and droppings each day. A single sign rarely identifies a cause. Compare changes with the individual’s usual behaviour. Record when a change began, recent foods, new birds and possible household exposures. Do not wait for a bird to look severely ill before asking for advice.

## Weigh without turning it into a struggle

A suitable scale can help track change when weighing is safe and the bird is comfortable. Use a stable setup and a consistent routine; discuss how to interpret the trend with a veterinarian. A number alone does not establish body condition, and comparing unrelated species or individuals is not a diagnosis.

## Feet and perches

Look for changes in grip, sores, swelling or reluctance to use a foot. Offer safe stable perches of suitable varied diameters and clean soiled areas. Check toys and fibres for entrapment. Do not perform invasive home treatment, cut tight material against the skin blindly or force a painful foot to grip.

## Beak changes and urgent signs

Difficulty eating, injury or a new change in beak appearance deserves assessment. Do not trim a beak as routine home care or assume overgrowth is only cosmetic. Breathing difficulty, collapse, uncontrolled bleeding or suspected poisoning need urgent professional attention. Bring observations and prior records, but do not delay transport for perfect notes.

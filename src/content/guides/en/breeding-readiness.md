## Three different questions

Sexual maturity asks when reproduction becomes biologically possible. Recommended breeding age asks when a healthy bird should first be considered for planned breeding. Individual readiness asks whether this particular bird, pair and household are prepared now. These are not interchangeable. A first egg can occur before a welfare-conscious breeding plan would be appropriate.

## Know the actual age

Ask for a hatch date or a clearly stated uncertainty, not simply “adult” or “breeding ready”. Keep identification and parentage records. Feather colour, cere appearance, eye appearance, song and mating attempts are not universal clocks. Species and mutations differ. If birth records are absent, discuss the limits of age assessment rather than inventing an exact birthday.

## Watch the pair, not just the nest

Sitting together is not enough to establish compatibility. Check that both birds can eat and rest without repeated displacement. Food guarding, repeated escape attempts or injury need attention before adding a nest. Separate birds safely when necessary; a nest box does not repair an incompatible pairing.

## The readiness checklist

Before offering a nest, confirm: known identity and age; suitable adult body condition; normal appetite and activity; an appropriate balanced feeding plan; no unexplained illness or active recovery; compatible unrelated parents; enough space and a separation option; a plan for chicks and emergencies. Arrange bird-experienced veterinary assessment. Finish quarantine and resolve health uncertainties before pairing.

## Reasons to postpone

Postpone when age or ancestry is unknown, one partner is frightened or excluded, body condition is poor, the bird is ill or recovering, or care depends on selling chicks quickly. Molting and repeated laying also deserve review of demands and recovery. A nest box, extra light or a supplement is not a shortcut to readiness. Avoid guessed “breeding booster” remedies.

## Eggs without a male

A female can lay infertile eggs without a male. This is not proof that a mate or nest should be supplied. Repeated laying, weakness, straining or altered breathing need veterinary advice. Do not manipulate an egg, press the abdomen or prescribe calcium by guesswork. Ask the veterinarian about a safe plan for the existing eggs and the bird’s reproductive management.

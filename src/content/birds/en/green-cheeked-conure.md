## Identity and varieties

A South American conure. Pineapple and turquoise are mutations of this species. A mutation label should never be given a separate scientific species page or used to promise a sweeter temperament.

Natural ancestry: South America. This is not proof of where an offered bird was born. Ask for accurate identification, hatch and health records. A market name alone may confuse species, mutations and domestic breeds.

## Set realistic expectations before acquisition

A small green-cheeked conure does not create the same noise or workload as a budgie. Affection, play and training can coexist with sharp calls, biting and destructive chewing. Pineapple or turquoise colour does not guarantee a quieter temperament.

Sun, Jenday and Nanday conures are different species. Establish the actual species, independent feeding, age evidence and previous habits. Amateur care of an unweaned chick is not a shortcut to bonding.

## The first week and the household

Begin with familiar food and a stable routine. Observe eating, rest and awareness without insisting that every offered hand be accepted. Maintain separate accommodation and a health plan before introductions.

Different household members can calmly participate in feeding or training. Constant body contact with one person is not the only form of social care. Allow both voluntary contact and independent occupation.

## Living space and companionship

Offer daily exploration, safe chewing and foraging with supervision. “Quieter than some conures” is not the same as quiet. Respect withdrawal and avoid turning playful beak use into forced wrestling or punishment.

Provide secure usable space rather than decorative crowding, stable perches where suitable, clean feeding areas and an undisturbed retreat. Supervise introductions; keep newcomers separate with a veterinary screening plan. Never rely on body size alone to predict compatibility.

## Housing, chewing and safe flight

Provide robust housing, secure latches, usable perches and flight room. Inspect soft plastic, rust, damaged coatings, loose parts and fraying cord. Match enrichment to the bird’s chewing strength.

Safe shredding, foraging and short target games provide occupation. Secure fans, doors and windows before exercise. Do not stop supervising contact with cats, dogs or larger parrots merely because they appear friendly.

## Feeding and daily care

Use a species-appropriate balanced feeding plan, not a bowl made mainly of favourite seeds. Introduce suitable vegetables and other foods gradually while observing actual intake. A bowl of husks can look full. Never withhold familiar food to force pellet conversion; ask a bird-experienced veterinarian about the individual’s needs.

Change drinking water and clean bowls whenever soiled. Keep a predictable rest routine and offer suitable bathing without force. Observe activity, appetite and droppings every day. Clean spoiled food and damp waste promptly; safe ventilation matters in Bangladesh’s humid weather.

## Feeding and cleanup in practice

Fruit preference does not make fruit the staple. Plan balanced conure food, suitable vegetables and limited rewards. Confirm that new foods are eaten; shredding is not proof of consumption.

Watery produce can increase the liquid component of droppings. Consider form, the usual pattern and other symptoms together. Plan washable protection and regular cleanup for thrown food, shredded toys and favourite perches.

## Droppings, odour and cleaning

Many household surfaces can be soiled during exercise. Place washable protection below favourite perches and keep food-preparation areas separate. Check the usual appearance of feces, urates and urine rather than expecting every dropping to look identical. A sudden persistent increase in liquid or a new strong smell needs attention, not perfume.

Food, water intake, stress and species can affect the urine component. Formed feces surrounded by more urine is not the same as loss of fecal form. Colour alone cannot diagnose illness. Persistent changes, especially with reduced appetite, lethargy or breathing difficulty, need veterinary assessment.

## Handling and health

Use voluntary, reward-based interaction and allow withdrawal. Not every species wants hand contact. Never chase, punish or promise speech. Sudden behavior changes, poor intake or persistent droppings changes warrant assessment; collapse, major bleeding, suspected toxins or breathing difficulty are urgent.

## Understand biting before training

Exploratory beak use, overexcited play and defensive biting differ. Observe tension, withdrawal and territory defence, including what happened immediately beforehand. Repeatedly ignoring limits can strengthen the problem.

Reward small voluntary steps onto a stable perch, stationing or target touches. When arousal rises, first establish distance and choose an easier task. Do not shake, frighten, punish or clamp the beak.

## Calls, rest and independent play

Calling can accompany waking, social contact or excitement. Quieter than some conures does not mean silent. Consider what is reinforced if attention or food arrives only after screaming; also reward calm contact and provide independent occupation.

A new toy alone may not solve boredom, unpredictable routines or disrupted sleep. Balance rest, contact and activities. A sudden change in behaviour or sound also deserves attention for possible illness.

## Pairing, nesting and parent welfare

**Sexual maturity is not a recommended breeding age.** A first egg, mating behaviour or a seller calling a bird “adult” is not enough. Use a known hatch date, an appropriate species-specific age assessment, healthy body condition and a compatible unrelated pair.

External appearance does not reliably sex every individual. Verify parentage and avoid related pairings when breeding colour mutations. A bonded pair or nest interest does not establish adult physical readiness.

## Eggs, chicks and warning signs

Before providing a nest, plan for parent health, daily unobtrusive observation, veterinary access and a suitable destination for every chick. More eggs are not a welfare target. Do not force pairing or keep an exhausted bird breeding.

Straining, weakness, unusual posture, sitting low or breathing difficulty need prompt veterinary assessment. Do not massage an abdomen, manipulate an egg or give a guessed supplement dose. A chick that is weak or not being fed needs experienced help; amateur syringe feeding risks serious injury. Choose fully weaned birds.

## Before bringing one home

Check daily care time, household noise tolerance, safe space, ongoing costs and who will care during travel. Look at individual behavior and records instead of a colour or breed label. See the first-bird and acquisition guides for a checklist.

## Common questions

### Is pineapple colour more affectionate?
Colour does not guarantee temperament. Individual history, upbringing and experiences with people matter.

### Is sun-conure care identical?
Some general parrot care overlaps, but species, calls, body form and habits differ. Read the sun-conure profile too.

### Does shoulder access create tameness?
Safe removal can be harder from a shoulder. First establish voluntary step-up/down from a low stable position and respect withdrawal.

### What if the bird will spend much of the day alone?
Plan realistic contact, occupation and backup care before acquisition. Another bird is not an automatic solution to social problems.

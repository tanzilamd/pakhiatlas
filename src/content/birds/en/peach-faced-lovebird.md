## Identity and varieties

Also known as the rosy-faced lovebird. It is distinct from Fischer’s and masked lovebirds. Colour names alone do not prove species identity; keep mutation and ancestry information separate.

Natural ancestry: Southwestern Africa. This is not proof of where an offered bird was born. Ask for accurate identification, hatch and health records. A market name alone may confuse species, mutations and domestic breeds.

## Arrival and first introductions

Peach-faced, Fischer’s and masked lovebirds are not interchangeable colour forms. Obtain identity, hatch information, independent-feeding history and the previous diet. Keep newcomers separate; placing one straight into an occupied cage is not a safe introduction.

Respect the bird’s willingness to approach and its enclosure territory. Build voluntary exit rather than forcing a hand into the cage. Moving separate cages closer and arranging supervised meetings must follow the birds’ behaviour, not a fixed promise of compatibility.

## Living space and companionship

A strong beak and territorial behavior can injure cage mates. Provide safe shredding material and watch for guarding of food or resting places. Do not place finches, budgies or an unfamiliar lovebird in the enclosure as an instant companion.

Provide secure usable space rather than decorative crowding, stable perches where suitable, clean feeding areas and an undisturbed retreat. Supervise introductions; keep newcomers separate with a veterinary screening plan. Never rely on body size alone to predict compatibility.

## Housing and safe occupation

A small body still has a powerful beak. Inspect latches, wire, plastic and fraying material. Leave flight room and offer safe paper or bird-appropriate chewing and foraging materials without crowding every space with toys.

If breeding is not intended, dark huts or nest boxes need not be provided as decoration. Check cloth and toys for entanglement. During exercise, secure fans, windows, hot objects and other pets.

## Feeding and daily care

Use a species-appropriate balanced feeding plan, not a bowl made mainly of favourite seeds. Introduce suitable vegetables and other foods gradually while observing actual intake. A bowl of husks can look full. Never withhold familiar food to force pellet conversion; ask a bird-experienced veterinarian about the individual’s needs.

Change drinking water and clean bowls whenever soiled. Keep a predictable rest routine and offer suitable bathing without force. Observe activity, appetite and droppings every day. Clean spoiled food and damp waste promptly; safe ventilation matters in Bangladesh’s humid weather.

## Daily feeding and cleanup

Use a balanced small-parrot plan rather than favourite seeds or sunflower alone. Introduce washed vegetables alongside familiar food. For two birds, check that both eat; falling total bowl contents do not prove equal access.

Inspect husks, wet food, shredded toys and waste beneath paper. Do not dismiss a new strong odour as an inherent lovebird smell. Assess cleanliness and illness signs rather than masking it with fragrance or herbs.

## Droppings, odour and cleaning

Many household surfaces can be soiled during exercise. Place washable protection below favourite perches and keep food-preparation areas separate. Check the usual appearance of feces, urates and urine rather than expecting every dropping to look identical. A sudden persistent increase in liquid or a new strong smell needs attention, not perfume.

Food, water intake, stress and species can affect the urine component. Formed feces surrounded by more urine is not the same as loss of fecal form. Colour alone cannot diagnose illness. Persistent changes, especially with reduced appetite, lethargy or breathing difficulty, need veterinary assessment.

## Handling and health

Use voluntary, reward-based interaction and allow withdrawal. Not every species wants hand contact. Never chase, punish or promise speech. Sudden behavior changes, poor intake or persistent droppings changes warrant assessment; collapse, major bleeding, suspected toxins or breathing difficulty are urgent.

## Company, biting and independence

A lovebird is not guaranteed to die simply because it is not kept as a breeding pair. Social needs remain important: a single bird increases the household’s responsibility for regular contact and occupation. Any two birds are not automatically a compatible pair.

Chasing, cornering or excluding a bird from food should not be dismissed as play. Be able to separate birds. Respect withdrawal with people too; do not punish biting with shouting, striking or holding the beak shut.

## Feathers, rest and habits

Provide a regular quiet night, voluntary bathing and safe daily movement. New feathers may be sensitive; do not forcibly remove sheaths. Feather damage or skin injury is not automatically solved by buying another bird.

Teach small voluntary steps: remaining nearby, accepting a reward and stepping onto a stable perch. Return to an easier step if fear develops and never train through food deprivation.

## Pairing, nesting and parent welfare

**Sexual maturity is not a recommended breeding age.** A first egg, mating behaviour or a seller calling a bird “adult” is not enough. Use a known hatch date, an appropriate species-specific age assessment, healthy body condition and a compatible unrelated pair.

Nest-material carrying is behavior, not proof of sex, maturity or readiness. Avoid pelvic palpation as a casual home test. Plan compatible unrelated parents, health assessment and chick destinations before breeding.

## Eggs, chicks and warning signs

Before providing a nest, plan for parent health, daily unobtrusive observation, veterinary access and a suitable destination for every chick. More eggs are not a welfare target. Do not force pairing or keep an exhausted bird breeding.

Straining, weakness, unusual posture, sitting low or breathing difficulty need prompt veterinary assessment. Do not massage an abdomen, manipulate an egg or give a guessed supplement dose. A chick that is weak or not being fed needs experienced help; amateur syringe feeding risks serious injury. Choose fully weaned birds.

## Before bringing one home

Check daily care time, household noise tolerance, safe space, ongoing costs and who will care during travel. Look at individual behavior and records instead of a colour or breed label. See the first-bird and acquisition guides for a checklist.

## Common questions

### Must bonded birds never be separated?
Bonds and separation distress matter, but leaving birds together when injury is likely is not a solution. Arrange safe accommodation and expert support where needed.

### Can two females behave like a pair?
Bonded behaviour does not establish sex. Breeding decisions require reliable identity, sex information and health assessment.

### Can a pet be kept without a nest box?
Yes. A nest box is not essential everyday equipment when breeding is not intended. Company, flight and occupation are more relevant.

### Can it share with smaller birds?
Neither size nor colour guarantees safety. Lovebirds can injure finches or quieter parakeets; separate secure enclosures are a conservative starting point.

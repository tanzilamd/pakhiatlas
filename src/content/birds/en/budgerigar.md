## Identity and varieties

A small Australian grass parakeet. English or exhibition budgies are domestic varieties of the same species; lutino, albino and other colours are not new species.

Natural ancestry: Australia.

## The first week: safety before friendship

Prepare housing, food, water and a retreat before arrival. Ask what the bird actually ate previously. Avoid passing it between people, catching it repeatedly or introducing another bird immediately. Watch from a distance for independent eating, balance and normal awareness.

Sit quietly nearby, offering a small reward at a comfortable distance rather than chasing with a hand. One confident bird does not set the pace for another. Separate accommodation and an initial veterinary plan come before social introductions.

## Living space and companionship

Provide room for repeated short flights and several safe perches, rather than filling a cage with toys. Offer suitable chewing and foraging tasks. A sociable budgie needs appropriate company; a mirror is not a substitute for a compatible companion or daily care.

## Check the room before flight

Close windows, balconies and outside doors, and stop fans completely. Help an unfamiliar bird recognise glass and mirrors through visible barriers and supervision. Remove open water, cooking hazards, hot containers, wires and access by cats or dogs. A kitchen is not a flight room.

Choose housing that prevents head/body entrapment and constant tail rubbing. Offer different safe perch shapes instead of only smooth plastic or sandpaper surfaces. Check toys for fraying and entanglement.

## A practical daily routine

In the morning, remove husks, confirm edible food, refresh water and observe overnight droppings and movement. Plan supervised flight, foraging and short training at a convenient time. Remove soft leftovers and clean bowls before a predictable, quiet and uninterrupted night’s rest.

Provide additional feeding positions if a confident bird excludes another. Include perch/toy inspection, dust removal and calm weight-trend recording in the routine. Do not fast or frighten a bird just to weigh it.

## Speech, mirrors and learning

Talking varies individually. Colour does not predict speech, and a companion bird does not automatically prevent taming. Chatter and social contact remain normal whether or not human words develop.

Keep a hand or low perch steady, reward voluntary approaches and finish after a small success. Avoid filling the daily diet with training treats. Change mirror use if fixation displaces eating or real social contact; a reflection is not a substitute for companionship.

## Sex, colour and pairing

The cere is the area around the nostrils above the beak. It can offer clues in some typical adult colours, but age, reproductive state and mutations complicate interpretation. Do not use a simple colour rule to definitively sex young, lutino, albino or some pied birds. Obtain reliable testing and records when a breeding or medical decision depends on sex.

English/exhibition budgies are a selected domestic form of the same species. Lutino, blue, spangle and pied describe colours or patterns. Normal movement, independent eating and care history matter more than matching colours.

## Pairing, nesting and parent welfare

[Before the nest: pairing and breeding readiness](../../guides/breeding-readiness/)

Cere appearance changes with age, reproductive state and some mutations, so one colour rule does not sex every budgie. An early egg or juvenile pairing is not evidence of safe breeding readiness. Keep hatch records and avoid nesting during poor condition or active illness.

## Common questions

### One bird or two?
A flock-minded bird needs adequate company. A single bird increases the household’s social responsibility; two birds are not guaranteed compatible. Plan health checks, gradual introductions and separate housing if needed.

### Can a full seed bowl conceal hunger?
Yes. Remove husks and check for edible seed, and watch whether another bird blocks access.

### Does wing clipping make a room safe?
It does not replace removing hazards. Do not make or perform that decision casually; discuss flight, behaviour and injury risks with an avian veterinarian.

### What happens when I travel?
Leave written feeding, cleaning, normal-behaviour and veterinary information with a responsible caregiver. Avoid starting diet or housing experiments during your absence.

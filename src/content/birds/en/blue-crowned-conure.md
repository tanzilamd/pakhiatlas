## Recognising the species

Look for the blue-tinged head, green body, pale bare eye area and long tail in the photograph. Older writing also uses Aratinga acuticaudata. This is a different species from pineapple or turquoise green-cheeked conures; the word conure does not imply identical behaviour or housing needs.

Its natural range is in South America. When considering captive care, ask for documented captive-bred origin, age and care history. A market label or photograph does not establish lawful provenance.

## Fit with the household

Do not plan for a soft-voiced bird. Listen to the calls beforehand when possible and consider neighbours and household tolerance. Observe communication and behaviour instead of deciding on a promise of speech.

Provide human contact alongside independent play, foraging and rest. A mirror or another bird does not automatically compensate for being left alone.

## Housing, enrichment and safe movement

Allow usable space for wings, tail and turning. Assess sturdy construction, secure latches and appropriate bar gaps. Do not fill the central movement area with branches and toys; check perch surfaces and chewing materials regularly.

Stop fans and secure windows, doors and other animals before flight. Keep the bird separate from kitchens, overheated nonstick cookware, incense, cigarettes and sprays. Remove threads and damaged clips that could trap a neck or foot.

## Food and everyday care

Plan a species-appropriate balanced diet with suitable vegetables and limited extras. Avoid selective eating of favourite seeds, and never withhold familiar food to force a new diet. Check actual intake even when the bowl looks full.

Wash and replace soiled water and bowls. Remove leftover fresh food, husks and chewed fragments. Offer bathing without force and keep a predictable quiet resting routine.

## Training and other birds

Reward voluntary approach, stepping onto a perch and short training tasks. Pause when the bird withdraws or uses its beak to ask for distance. Punishment, chasing and forced shoulder contact do not build trust.

Plan health screening and separate accommodation for newcomers. Introduction does not mean immediate cage sharing. Chasing, blocking food or attempts to bite feet require secure separate housing.

## Reproduction and health observation

An adult label, pair bond or attractive colour is not enough reason to add a nest. Consider known age and ancestry, health, compatibility and a plan for every chick. Avoid repeated reproductive pressure.

Learn the usual feces, white urates and liquid urine in droppings. Cleanup also includes scattered food and chewing debris. Do not dismiss a new strong smell or persistent change as normal for the species. Reduced eating, weakness or breathing difficulty needs prompt veterinary attention.

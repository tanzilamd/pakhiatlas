## Identity and varieties

A crested Australian parrot with contact calls and feather dust. Lutino, pearl and pied are colour varieties, not separate species. Singing or a yellow face is not a universal sexing rule across ages and mutations.

Natural ancestry: Australia.

## Building comfort in the first week

Start with familiar food, accessible water and stable perches. An alert raised crest in a new room is not an invitation to pet immediately. Observe eating, droppings and rest without simultaneously changing food, companionship and training.

Calm contact can begin while a separate-accommodation health plan is maintained. Even a familiar cockatiel may not enjoy head touching. Allow voluntary approach, withdrawal and rest.

## Living space and companionship

Keep the sleeping area predictable and remove collision hazards. If a bird startles at night, observe the trigger rather than repeatedly grabbing it. Offer safe flight and gentle voluntary interaction; assess household tolerance of dust before acquiring one.

[Housing & a bird-safe home](../../guides/safe-home/)

## Prepare the room and perches

Leave usable space for the crest, wings and long tail. Do not block movement with too many toys. Plan bowl and perch positions together so droppings do not fall into water.

Stop fans, secure doors/windows and help the bird recognise glass. Separate it from cooking fumes, overheated nonstick cookware, incense, smoke, sprays and strong fragrances. Manage feather powder with appropriate cleaning and safe ventilation rather than perfume.

## Turn feeding into a workable routine

Check water and edible food in the morning. Offer suitable vegetables alongside the familiar staple and verify actual eating. Include supervised movement, short training and independent occupation. Remove soft leftovers before spoilage.

Covering a cage beside a noisy bright television does not guarantee good sleep. Provide a predictable quiet environment; any cover must allow ventilation and not increase fear. For night panic, stabilise lighting and the environment, then assess injury rather than chasing a flapping bird.

## Feathers, bathing and body language

Developing feathers can be sensitive. Do not pull sheaths or force body contact. Offer clean shallow bathing water or a gentle spray if welcomed; avoid soap, perfume and hair dryers.

Read crest, eyes, feather position, tension and withdrawal together. A crest alone does not prove anger or happiness. A lowered head may invite brief scratching, but repeated back/body stroking should not become the routine.

## Whistling, biting and trust

Speech or imitation is not the sole training goal. Song does not guarantee sex or future talking. Notice withdrawal or tension before a bite; cornering a bird to force step-up can increase fear and biting.

Reward voluntary movement toward a steady hand or low perch, finish after small successes and build independent play. Calm relationships with more than one caregiver are more sustainable than total dependency on one person.

## Pairing, nesting and parent welfare

[Before the nest: pairing and breeding readiness](../../guides/breeding-readiness/)

Females can lay eggs without a male. Repeated laying deserves veterinary discussion even when the bird looks active. Do not add a nest box to solve loneliness, and do not remove or replace eggs through an improvised plan that may encourage more laying.

## Common questions

### Can a lutino be sexed by colour alone?
No. Some typical-grey visual cues do not apply across mutations, and song is not a definitive test. Obtain appropriate testing records if the decision requires certainty.

### Does cuttlebone mean grit is needed too?
Soluble calcium sources and insoluble stone grit are different. Cockatiels generally do not need stone grit for digestion. Discuss the overall diet and individual needs with a veterinarian.

### Can a bird lay without a mate?
Yes. Repeated laying needs health and environmental assessment, not automatically adding a male or nest box.

### What about household dust sensitivity?
Powder down is a real part of cockatiel care. Consider household sensitivity and medical advice before choosing; no bird is guaranteed allergy-safe.

## Colour and identity

Blue wings and back, a yellow breast, green forehead and pale facial area help identify this macaw. It is also called the blue-and-gold macaw. It is not a colour form of a small blue budgie, and not every bird called macaw belongs to the same species.

Its natural range is tropical South America. Before acquisition, check the species, hatch records, identification and lawful documented captive origin. Market presence or somebody already keeping one does not establish legality.

## Decisions to make first

This bird needs experienced care and substantial space. Loud calls, daily contact, movement and frequent replacement chewing materials must fit the household routine. Beak strength makes direct contact with children or other animals a serious consideration.

Plan sustained care, veterinary transport and skilled backup during absence. Promises of speech or never biting should not determine a purchase.

## Housing and surroundings

Assess actual clear room for wings, the entire tail and turning. A cage that looks large for a small bird is not automatically suitable for a macaw. Sturdy construction, secure latches, appropriate perches and routine inspection matter.

Replace safe chewing material as needed. Remove broken metal, flaking paint, sharp fragments and trapping hazards. Manage fans, doors, windows, electrical cables and other animals during movement. Keep fumes and sprays away.

## Feeding, debris and cleanup

Plan a balanced diet appropriate to the species and individual condition. Do not fill the diet with nuts, sunflower or favourite seeds; being able to eat nuts does not mean unlimited portions. Introduce changes gradually and observe intake and condition.

Large washable bowls and floor protection are practical. Allow for husks, scattered vegetables, chewed wood and droppings together. Make the area under favourite perches easy to clean from the start.

## Companionship, training and rest

Teach small voluntary steps such as stepping onto a perch, moving to a station and being comfortable near a carrier. Reward participation rather than forcing handling or punishing biting. Do not hand the bird to an inexperienced visitor to try handling it.

Provide independent activity and quiet rest alongside social contact. Constant shoulder time or intense physical affection is not the only sign of a good relationship. Size and beak strength create particular risks around other birds; maintain secure separate housing.

## Health, reproduction and acquisition

Observe eating, movement, normal calls and droppings daily. Recognise feces, urates and urine; colour alone cannot identify disease. Arrange urgent assessment for breathing difficulty, major bleeding, weakness or suspected toxic exposure.

A large bird and a nest box are not a complete breeding arrangement. Parent health, ancestry records, separation space, experienced help and a future for every chick are needed. Do not buy an unweaned chick to learn syringe feeding.
